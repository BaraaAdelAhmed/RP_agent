\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:formulation}

This section states the physical model, the boundary-value problem it leads to, and the observables that the rest of
the paper computes. The model combines three ingredients that have so far been treated separately: a permeable
particle, a permeable wall, and unsteady fluid inertia.

\subsection{Geometry, kinematics and assumptions}\label{sec:geometry}
We consider a spherical core--shell particle of outer radius $b$ whose concentric, impermeable core has radius $a=\kappa
b$ (\cref{fig:schematic}). The core ratio $0\le\kappa\le1$ spans the homogeneous porous sphere ($\kappa=0$) and the
impermeable sphere ($\kappa=1$). The particle is immersed in an incompressible Newtonian fluid of density $\rho$ and
dynamic viscosity $\mu$, which fills the half-space $z>0$ outside the particle (domain~1). The permeable shell
$a<r<b$ (domain~2) and the half-space $z<0$ (domain~3) are porous media saturated with the same fluid. Each is
characterised by a Darcy permeability, $k_s$ for the shell and $k_p$ for the layer, which measures the ease of flow
through its pores. Each also has a Brinkman (effective) viscosity, $\mu_s$ and $\mu_p$, which governs momentum
diffusion inside it. Domain~3 represents a glycocalyx or mucus layer whose thickness exceeds the range of the
disturbance. The particle centre lies at $\bx_0=h\be_z$, a height $h>b$ above the layer, and $(r,\theta,\varphi)$
denote spherical coordinates centred at $\bx_0$. The dimensionless gap $\varepsilon=(h-b)/b$ measures the clearance
between the particle surface and the layer.

The particle translates without rotating at the velocity $\operatorname{Re}[U\be\,e^{-i\omega t}]$, where $\omega$ is
the angular frequency, $U$ the complex velocity amplitude, and the unit vector $\be$ equals $\be_z$ for normal
oscillation and $\be_x$ for parallel oscillation. Every field is written as the real part of a complex amplitude times
$e^{-i\omega t}$, and we use the same symbol for a field and its amplitude. The analysis rests on five assumptions.

\begin{assumption}[Small amplitude]\label{as:linear}
The displacement amplitude $|U|/\omega$ is small compared with $b$ and with the gap $h-b$. Convective inertia is then
negligible against unsteady inertia, the boundary conditions apply at the mean particle position, and the problem
is linear and time-harmonic.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Rigid porous matrices]\label{as:rigid}
The solid matrices are rigid: the shell matrix moves with the particle and the layer matrix is at rest. ``Soft'' thus
refers to hydrodynamic permeability, as in soft-particle electrokinetics, not to elastic deformation, which is treated
for impermeable elastic walls in Ref.~\onlinecite{batalha2026elasto}.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Brinkman media with stress jump]\label{as:brinkman}
Flow in each porous region obeys the unsteady Brinkman equation, with a Darcy drag proportional to the velocity relative
to the local matrix. Momentum transfer across each fluid--porous surface follows the Ochoa-Tapia--Whitaker (OTW)
stress-jump condition~\cite{ochoatapia1995momentum}.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Prescribed translation]\label{as:norotation}
The particle is driven in pure translation, as a colloidal probe glued to an AFM cantilever or a particle in an imposed
acoustic field, so that no torque-free rotation is allowed.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Thick layer]\label{as:thick}
The layer is a half-space, which is appropriate when its thickness exceeds both its Brinkman screening length
$\sqrt{k_p\mu_p/\mu}$ and the height $h$.
\end{assumption}

\subsection{Governing equations and interface conditions}\label{sec:equations}
Under \cref{as:linear,as:rigid,as:brinkman}, the clear fluid obeys the unsteady Stokes equations, while the shell and the
layer obey unsteady Brinkman equations in which the Darcy drag $\mu/k$ acts on the velocity relative to the matrix:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:governing}
\begin{align}
  -i\omega\rho\,\bu^{(1)}&=-\nabla p^{(1)}+\mu\nabla^2\bu^{(1)}, \label{eq:stokes}\\
  -i\omega\rho\,\bu^{(2)}&=-\nabla p^{(2)}+\mu_s\nabla^2\bu^{(2)}-\frac{\mu}{k_s}\left(\bu^{(2)}-U\be\right),\\
  -i\omega\rho\,\bu^{(3)}&=-\nabla p^{(3)}+\mu_p\nabla^2\bu^{(3)}-\frac{\mu}{k_p}\,\bu^{(3)},
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
with $\nabla\cdot\bu^{(j)}=0$ in every domain. Taking the curl of \cref{eq:governing} shows that the vorticity in domain
$j$ satisfies $(\nabla^2-\lambda_j^2)\curl\bu^{(j)}=\bm 0$, with complex screening parameters
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_f^2=-\frac{i\omega\rho}{\mu},\qquad
  \lambda_s^2=\frac{\mu/k_s-i\omega\rho}{\mu_s},\qquad
  \lambda_p^2=\frac{\mu/k_p-i\omega\rho}{\mu_p},\qquad\operatorname{Re}\lambda_j>0 .
  \label{eq:lambdas}
\end{equation}
In the fluid, $1/\operatorname{Re}\lambda_f=\delta_v=\sqrt{2\mu/\rho\omega}$ is the viscous penetration depth, the
distance over which vorticity diffuses during one oscillation period. In the porous media the decay of vorticity is
set jointly by the Darcy drag and the unsteady inertia, whichever is stronger. The stress tensor is
$\bsig^{(j)}=-p^{(j)}\bI+\mu_j(\nabla\bu^{(j)}+\nabla\bu^{(j)\mathsf T})$, with $\mu_1=\mu$, $\mu_2=\mu_s$ and
$\mu_3=\mu_p$.

The conditions at the three surfaces are written with the outward unit normal $\bn$ and the tangential projector
$\bm\Pi=\bI-\bn\bn$. On the core surface $r=a$, the fluid cannot penetrate the core and slips along it with the Navier
slip length $\ell_c$, which models a hydrophobic core:
\begin{equation}
  \left(\bu^{(2)}-U\be\right)\cdot\bn=0,\qquad
  \bm\Pi\cdot\left(\bu^{(2)}-U\be\right)=\frac{\ell_c}{\mu_s}\,\bm\Pi\cdot\bsig^{(2)}\cdot\bn .
  \label{eq:bc_core}
\end{equation}
On the shell surface $r=b$, velocity and normal stress are continuous, whereas the tangential stress jumps:
\begin{equation}
  \bu^{(1)}=\bu^{(2)},\qquad \bn\cdot\bsig^{(1)}\cdot\bn=\bn\cdot\bsig^{(2)}\cdot\bn,\qquad
  \bm\Pi\cdot\left(\bsig^{(2)}-\bsig^{(1)}\right)\cdot\bn=\frac{\beta_s\mu}{\sqrt{k_s}}\,\bm\Pi\cdot\left(\bu^{(1)}-U\be\right).
  \label{eq:bc_shell}
\end{equation}
The stress jump expresses the momentum exchanged with the solid matrix inside the thin transition zone between the
clear fluid and the porous medium. Its sign and magnitude are set by the dimensionless coefficient $\beta_s$, and it
vanishes for $\beta_s=0$~\cite{ochoatapia1995momentum}. For an impermeable particle ($\kappa=1$) the shell is absent and
\cref{eq:bc_shell} is replaced by no penetration and Navier slip of length $\ell_c$ on $r=b$, which is the slip sphere of
Ref.~\onlinecite{faltas2021axisymmetric}. On the layer surface $z=0$ the same continuity conditions and an OTW jump with
coefficient $\beta_p$ apply:
\begin{equation}
  \bu^{(1)}=\bu^{(3)},\qquad \sigma^{(1)}_{zz}=\sigma^{(3)}_{zz},\qquad
  \sigma^{(3)}_{\tau z}-\sigma^{(1)}_{\tau z}=\frac{\beta_p\mu}{\sqrt{k_p}}\,u^{(1)}_\tau\quad(\tau=x,y).
  \label{eq:bc_plane}
\end{equation}
All disturbances decay far from the particle, in the fluid and in the layer.

\begin{remark}[Limits of the layer]\label{rem:bj}
As $k_p\to0$ the layer velocity vanishes and \cref{eq:bc_plane} becomes no slip on a rigid impermeable wall, the
configuration of Zhang \emph{et al.}~\cite{zhang2023unsteady}. For $\mu_p=\mu$, $\beta_p=0$ and $k_p\to\infty$ the layer
cannot be distinguished from the fluid and the interface disappears. For a Darcy-dominated layer, eliminating the porous
side yields an effective slip condition of Beavers--Joseph type~\cite{beavers1967boundary}, whose coefficient depends on
$\beta_p$ and $\mu_p/\mu$. \TODO{authors: decide whether to add the explicit reduction, cf.\
Ref.~\onlinecite{elsayed2026effect}}
\end{remark}

\subsection{Dimensionless problem, force decomposition and observables}\label{sec:nondim}
Scaling lengths by $b$, velocities by $U$, stresses by $\mu U/b$ and forces by $\mu bU$ leaves the groups of
\cref{tab:groups}. The Womersley number $\Wo=b\sqrt{\rho\omega/\mu}$ compares the particle radius with the viscous
penetration depth, whereas $\alpha_s=b/\sqrt{k_s}$ and $\alpha_p=b/\sqrt{k_p}$ compare it with the Darcy screening lengths
of the shell and the layer. In these units the screening parameters \eqref{eq:lambdas} become
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_fb=e^{-i\pi/4}\Wo,\qquad
  (\lambda_sb)^2=\frac{\alpha_s^2-i\Wo^2}{\mu_s/\mu},\qquad
  (\lambda_pb)^2=\frac{\alpha_p^2-i\Wo^2}{\mu_p/\mu},
  \label{eq:lambdas_nd}
\end{equation}
which shows that unsteady inertia overtakes the Darcy drag in the layer once $\Wo\gtrsim\alpha_p$ and in the shell once
$\Wo\gtrsim\alpha_s$.

By symmetry, the hydrodynamic force $\oint_{r=b}\bsig^{(1)}\cdot\bn\,\dd S$ is parallel to $\be$, and we write its
amplitude as $F=F_\infty+F_{\rm int}$. Here $F_\infty$ is the force in an unbounded fluid and $F_{\rm int}$ the
interaction force induced by the layer. The interaction force has a dissipative part in phase with the velocity and an
inertial part in phase with the acceleration:
\begin{equation}
  F_{\rm int}=-\gamma_{\rm int}\,U-m_{\rm int}\,\frac{\dd U}{\dd t}
  =\left(-\gamma_{\rm int}+i\omega m_{\rm int}\right)U .
  \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
The wall-induced damping $\gamma_{\rm int}$ measures the extra energy dissipated because of the layer. The wall-induced
added mass $m_{\rm int}$ is the change, caused by the layer, in the mass of fluid that is effectively accelerated with
the particle; it may be negative. Their dimensionless forms are
\begin{equation}
  \hat\gamma_{\rm int}=-\frac{\operatorname{Re}\hat F_{\rm int}}{6\pi},\qquad
  \hat\chi_{\rm int}=\frac{\operatorname{Im}\hat F_{\rm int}}{6\pi}=\frac{\omega m_{\rm int}}{6\pi\mu b},\qquad
  \frac{m_{\rm int}}{m_f}=\frac{9}{2}\,\frac{\hat\chi_{\rm int}}{\Wo^2},
  \label{eq:observables}
\end{equation}
with $\hat F=F/(\mu bU)$ and $m_f=\tfrac43\pi\rho b^3$ the displaced fluid mass. The reactive coefficient
$\hat\chi_{\rm int}$ carries the sign of $m_{\rm int}$. We also report the phase $\phi=\arg(-F/U)$ of the total
resistance.

\begin{remark}[Sign convention and AFM frequency shift]\label{rem:sign}
With \cref{eq:decomposition}, a positive $m_{\rm int}$ adds inertia. For a probe of effective mass $M^*$ on a spring,
the resonance frequency therefore shifts by $\Delta\omega_0/\omega_0\simeq-m_{\rm int}/(2M^*)$ when the shift is small.
A change of $m_{\rm int}$ from negative to positive values as $\Wo$ increases is thus the change of the frequency
shift from positive to negative reported by Zhang \emph{et al.}~\cite{zhang2023unsteady}.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Crossover Womersley numbers]\label{def:woc}
The crossover Womersley numbers are the roots of $m_{\rm int}(\Wo)=0$ at fixed geometry and material parameters. A
root is \emph{upward}, $\Wo_c^{\uparrow}$, when $m_{\rm int}$ changes from negative to positive as $\Wo$ increases, and
\emph{downward}, $\Wo_c^{\downarrow}$, otherwise. A rigid sphere near a rigid wall has a single upward root, denoted
$\Wo_c$.
\end{definition}

The task is to compute $F_{\rm int}$, and from it $\hat\gamma_{\rm int}$, $m_{\rm int}$, $\phi$ and the crossover set,
over the ranges of \cref{tab:groups} for both directions of oscillation. Three features make this difficult. The
boundaries have incompatible symmetries, spherical at $r=a,b$ and planar at $z=0$, so no coordinate system separates
the problem, and bispherical coordinates do not accommodate a Brinkman half-space. Each domain also carries its own
complex screening length \eqref{eq:lambdas}, so the disturbance combines a long-range potential part decaying as $r^{-3}$
with a vortical part confined within $|\lambda_f|^{-1}$ of the particle, and the porous interface converts each part
into the other. Finally, the parameters span the transition from viscous to inertial flow and from Darcy-dominated to
inertia-dominated porous media, so that no single asymptotic expansion covers them. \Cref{sec:analysis} therefore
treats the sphere exactly in spherical harmonics and the plane exactly in Fourier space, and couples the two through
the dipolar moments of the flow, which alone determine the force.
